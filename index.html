LibLab Fellows — Project Reflection

Building a Virtual Exhibition: Lessons from the Field

What we learned attempting to make 150 years of international student history come alive — and accessible — online

By Germain Hirwa & Muhammad Saleh Haider  ·  Spring 2026  ·  Swarthmore College LibLab

When we set out to digitize the 150 Years of International Students @ Swarthmore exhibit, we believed we could unlock something genuinely new — an immersive, globally accessible archive that anyone could walk through, wherever they were in the world. What followed was a semester of honest reckoning with the limits of today's tools.

How it started

The project began as a solo project offered to Saleh, with a clear and ambitious goal: recreate the physical exhibition that had been on display in McCabe Library and bring it into a virtual space that could outlast its four walls. The platform of choice was Spatial.io, a browser-based virtual environment that allows creators to build 3D galleries viewable both on desktop and in VR headsets.

From the start, Saleh had been provided with a rich set of archival materials by Kathlyn Melving from the International Students Office — several folders of PDF documents from the International Student Center archives dating as far back as the 1980s. These formed the documentary backbone of the exhibit.

In mid-March, Germain joined the project to help ensure it could move quickly and land well. Together, we dove into the practical work of digitization — collecting 360-degree photographs and videos of the IC building using a 360-degree camera, assembling the archival documents, and beginning to construct the virtual space.

The challenges

What we ran into

We encountered significant obstacles in four distinct areas — each one narrowing what we could accomplish within Spatial.io's free tier.

01

360° video blocked by storage limits

The 360-degree videos we captured of the IC building couldn't be uploaded — Spatial.io's free tier rejected them for exceeding the data limit and prompted us to upgrade to a paid plan.

02

Multi-page documents with no slideshow

More than 70% of the archival documents were multi-page. Since Spatial.io has no native slideshow feature, we had to manually duplicate and arrange individual page images — a workaround that produced inconsistent scales across the exhibit.

03

Platform instability

Every edit triggered a full page reload before we could make the next change. This seemingly small friction consumed a disproportionate amount of our time and made iterative design nearly impossible.

04

No meaningful accessibility features

Despite exploring every available option, the only accessibility feature we could find was the ability to add image descriptions. This left visitors with visual, motor, or cognitive disabilities without meaningful access to the exhibit.

Accessibility

The hardest conversation

Accessibility was never a secondary concern for us. It is a core value of the LibLab fellowship — and it should be a core value of any digital exhibit that claims to serve a broad public. So when Spatial.io's accessibility features proved almost nonexistent, we didn't move on. We kept looking.

Our Emerging Technologies Fellow, Liz Hogrefe, connected us with colleagues at UT San Antonio who specialize in digital accessibility — Vanessa Garza (Learning Experience Accessibility Specialist) and RaLynn McGuire (Lead Accessibility/UDL Specialist, CPACC). The introduction came through Jen Moore, Academic Technologist for the Arts & Humanities at Swarthmore, who recognized that an outside expert perspective would be invaluable.

We met on April 16th for a focused thirty-minute consultation. Their expertise was immediately clarifying.

"We haven't been able to find a platform that offers both genuine accessibility features and an embedded VR experience — and we've been actively looking."

RaLynn McGuire & Vanessa Garza, UT San Antonio

This wasn't a critique of our specific implementation choices. It was a field-wide observation: the tools don't exist yet. No current VR/spatial platform meaningfully accommodates people with visual, motor, or cognitive disabilities within the immersive experience itself. The state of the art, for now, is to build a separate, fully accessible website alongside the VR experience — a companion that doesn't depend on the spatial environment at all.

Key finding

Spatial.io, in its current form, has no features to accommodate visitors with disabilities. Making an exhibit built in Spatial.io meaningfully accessible would require building an entirely separate website with accessibility features baked in from the ground up.

Looking ahead

What we'd tell the next team

We leave this project with a clearer map of the terrain. For anyone considering a similar virtual exhibition project, here is what we learned:

  • Audit your platform's data limits before collecting media at scale — especially for rich formats like 360° video.
  • Assume multi-page documents will be the norm in archival work and confirm your platform can handle them before committing to it.
  • Treat accessibility as a platform selection criterion, not a post-launch add-on. Ask: what can a screen reader user actually do here?
  • If the VR experience cannot be made accessible, budget time and resources to build a companion web experience that can.
  • Spatial.io is not the right fit for virtual exhibition projects that need to be interactive, stable, and accessible — at least not at the free tier.

The history we were working to preserve — 150 years of international students at Swarthmore — deserves to be told to everyone. That aspiration will outlast any single platform.
